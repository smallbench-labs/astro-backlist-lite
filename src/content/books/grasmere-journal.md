    May 14th, 1800._--Wm. and John set off into Yorkshire after dinner at half-past two o'clock, cold pork in their pockets. I left them at the turning of the Low-wood bay under the trees. My heart was so full that I could hardly speak to W. when I gave him a farewell kiss. I sate a long time upon a stone at the margin of the lake, and after a flood of tears my heart was easier. The lake looked to me, I knew not why, dull and melancholy, and the weltering on the shores seemed a heavy sound. I walked as long as I could amongst the stones of the shore. The wood rich in flowers; a beautiful yellow (palish yellow) flower, that looked thick, round, and double--the smell very sweet (I supposed it was a ranunculus), crowfoot, the grassy-leaved rabbit-looking white flower, strawberries, geraniums, scentless violets, anemones, two kinds of orchises, primroses, the heckberry very beautiful, the crab coming out as a low shrub. Met an old man, driving a very large beautiful bull, and a cow. He walked with two sticks. Came home by Clappersgate. […]

The journal begins on the day William and John Wordsworth leave for Yorkshire and Dorothy, alone at Dove Cottage, resolves to keep a record “because I shall give William pleasure by it when he comes home again”. It runs to January 1803, through William's marriage, and is full of exact things: what grew where, who came to the door, the moon on the lake, the day they found the daffodils.

Knight's edition was the first to print the Grasmere journal at length. We reproduce it as he published it — including his explanation of what he left out — rather than conflate it with later editions.
