By the door where the wall of the room was papered with old numbers of the *Morgenbladet*, I could distinguish clearly a notice from the Director of Lighthouses, and a little to the left of that an inflated advertisement of Fabian Olsens' new-baked bread.

    The instant I opened my eyes I began, from sheer force of habit, to think if I had anything to rejoice over that day. I had been somewhat hard-up lately, and one after the other of my belongings had been taken to my "Uncle." I had grown nervous and irritable. A few times I had kept my bed for the day with vertigo. An unnamed young man wanders the streets of Kristiania — now Oslo — selling articles to newspapers when he can, pawning his waistcoat when he cannot, chewing wood shavings to cheat his stomach. He lies to landladies, gives his last coins away, and writes all night on an article he is sure will make his name.

Nothing much happens in *Hunger*, and that was the point: Hamsun wanted a novel of the nerves rather than of events. Egerton, who knew Hamsun in Norway, translated it nine years after it appeared. This edition follows her text, restores passages cut from later reprints, and adds a note on the translation.
